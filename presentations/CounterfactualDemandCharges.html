This deck goes with the draft "Utility Bill Savings: Counterfactual Demand Charges from Simulated Billing-Period Peaks". It is part of Utility Bill Savings and replaces one step of it: the counterfactual's demand charges. Everything else about the bill stays the same.

The swings come from the model fitted on the whole baseline: observed minus predicted, hour by hour. Days are borrowed in runs of 7: the next baseline day is taken while it is still the same day type and its weather gap is within twice the gap of the 10th nearest day; otherwise a fresh day is drawn at random from the 10 nearest. The example's rows are made up to show the rule.

Step 3 of 5

Shift the whole month

A model can get a whole month wrong, not just an hour.

So the model is refit with each baseline month held out, then asked to predict that month. The gap between real and predicted average load is that month's level error.

Each simulation adds one of those errors to every hour of a billing period.

The held-out fits use 4 folds of interleaved weeks, so every month keeps three quarters of its days in each fit. For each baseline month, the level error is its real average hourly load minus the held-out prediction's average. A simulation draws one error at random from all baseline months for each billing period, and adds it to every hour of that period. In this example it lifts the period; another simulation might lower it.

Step 4 of 5

Scale the whole year

One baseline year cannot show how far a whole year can run above or below the model.

So each simulation gets one year factor, such as ×0.92 or ×1.08, that scales every hour.

The factor is 1 + r, with r drawn from a normal distribution centered on zero: a standard deviation of 8% for commercial buildings and 16% for homes, set on buildings with no intervention. It covers routine change in the building and error in the model itself. Because it is centered, it widens the range of peaks without moving the central value. One factor applies to every hour and every month of a simulation, which is why a year's range does not shrink by averaging months.

Step 5 of 5

Take each simulation's peak

Each simulation's highest interval in the hours a charge covers: the whole period for a flat charge, its own hours for a time-of-use charge.

Each peak is then calibrated on buildings with no intervention, so the range holds the real peak about 80% of the time.

Each peak is priced on the tariff. The bill uses the average of the 1,000 charges.

Five simulations are drawn here; the method runs 1,000. The calibration is fixed per segment: for commercial buildings each peak is multiplied by 0.96, because borrowed days give a simulation slightly more chances at an extreme hour than a real building has, and the spread is widened by 1.1. Homes use no correction and a 1.2 widening. Each calibrated peak is priced on the tariff, and the average of those charges is the counterfactual's demand charge.

One 30-day billing period, $15/kW flat demand charge · figures from the methodology's worked illustration

On this flat $15/kW charge, the average of the charges is the mean peak times the rate: 535 kW × $15 = $8,025. Priced on the average's peak, the counterfactual charge is $7,050 against a metered $7,800, a $750 loss for a building that changed nothing. Priced this way, the savings are +$225, with a range of −$600 to +$1,080 that includes zero. The mean, not the median, is billed because a bill is an expected value: monthly means add up to the expected annual total, and medians do not.

A tier is a step. A mean peak just over the step bills the whole step, although one simulation in five pays nothing; a mean just under it bills nothing at all. The average of the charges is right on both sides of the step. On a flat rate the two are equal, so the distinction only matters on tiered tariffs. A demand ratchet still uses the mean peak, because a ratchet compares one demand per period with earlier periods.

What each billing period reports

The billed charge

The average of the 1,000 simulated charges, with the mean peak beside it.

The range

P10 to P90 of the peak reduction and of the demand-charge savings.

A bound

The P95: the level the building would not have exceeded.

How predictable

A peak score from the baseline alone: high, medium or low. Shown beside the result, never a gate.

The year's total range is taken over the simulations, not by adding up monthly percentiles, because one year-over-year factor moves all twelve months of a simulation together. Every demand charge is covered: flat, time-of-use and coincident.

Where it holds, and where it does not yet

Holds

Commercial buildings, calibrated on buildings with no project.

Flat, time-of-use and coincident demand charges, with tiers and proration.

Hourly or 15-minute meter data, with bills or calendar months as billing periods.

Not yet

Homes: ranges are wide and less reliable.

Net-metered solar: a net meter hides the gross load.

15-minute calibration: the fixed factors were fitted on hourly data.

Bill the peak the building would have set, and say how sure we are.

Where the method cannot run, for example when too few held-out baseline fits succeed, the bill falls back to the standard counterfactual peak of Utility Bill Savings and the substitution is recorded. A per-building peak correction is the main item of future work.
