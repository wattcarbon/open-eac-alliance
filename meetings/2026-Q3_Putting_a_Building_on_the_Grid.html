Putting a building on the grid

Give us an address, and we look up three different kinds of information about the grid underneath it.

The output

What we work out about a site

Value streams — what a DER can earn
  • Energy arbitrageStore power when it is cheap, give it back when it is expensive.
  • Congestion reliefEase a pinch point on the network, in the hours it is actually pinched.
  • Marginal lossDeliver power near where it is used, so less of it is lost on the way.
  • Avoided emissionsThe CO2e never emitted, because something dirtier stayed off.
  • CapacityPaid to be there at the peak, so the grid can build less firm generation.
  • Ancillary servicesPaid to help hold the grid steady second to second.
  • T&D deferralPut off a wire, transformer or substation upgrade the utility would pay for.
  • Tariff savingsMoney off the customer's own bill, under their own rate plan.
Scores — what the site is like
  • Load scoreHow hard the local load peaks, and how much building stock sits nearby.
  • Congestion scoreHow much the local price swings when the network is constrained.
  • DER needWhich kind of flexibility the area is short of.

Three separate look-ups. Any one can come back empty while the other two answer.

One of three · a point

Nodal pricing

What it gives us
  • Energy arbitrage
  • Congestion relief
  • Marginal loss
  • Avoided emissions
One worked example
  • Mountain View, California — the nearest priced point is WHISMAN_1_N001, 1,282 m away
  • Reached along 27 conductor spans through 16 nodes, not in a straight line
  • It hangs off WHISMAN substation, 115 kV, carrying two lines
  • That node sits in the 91st percentile for congestion

The nearest priced point is the nearest one by cable, not the closest one on a map.

Two of three · a region

Regional value

What it gives us
  • Capacity
  • Ancillary services
  • T&D deferral
  • Tariff savings
One worked example
  • Mountain View, California — five territories cover it, and they overlap
  • Capacity alone is carried by four of them, at four different grains
  • The finest of those is local_area, so that is the one it is priced at

These prices cover a whole area, so what matters is which area you are in.

Three of three · an area

Local load

What it gives us
  • How much the area peaks
  • How much building stock is nearby
  • Which kind of DER would help
One worked example
  • Mountain View, California — the nearest substation is WHISMAN, 1,282 m away
  • 135,279 buildings sit within 12 km of it
  • Its load scores 82.6, the 73rd percentile
  • So the DER that suits it is non-HVAC flexibility, chosen from congestion

This one always answers. Every address has a nearest substation.

Examples

Three addresses

The same three look-ups, at three addresses. What comes back differs by market, not by effort.

All three answer
  • Mountain View, California — CAISO, priced at a node 1,282 m away, 91st percentile for congestion
  • WHISMAN substation, 115 kV · 135,279 buildings within 12 km
Priced, but for a whole zone
  • Discovery Green, Texas — ERCOT, priced at the HB_HOUSTON trading hub, not at a node
  • 54th percentile for congestion · POLK substation, 138 kV, 217 m away
No market, and still an answer
  • Atlanta, Georgia — inside Southern Company, so there is no market price to read
  • The load look-up still answers: 164,402 buildings, 40th percentile, load shifting the DER that suits it
